# SUNDIAL · La Meridiana

**Nine panels. One sun. Seven days in Bosa.**

For seven days around the summer solstice, nine old solar panels hang on walls across Bosa. They have finished their working lives on roofs. Now they have one job left: to watch the sun, and to make a picture of what they saw.

Each panel powers only itself. There is no battery, no cable and no internet. When the light comes up, the panel wakes. All day it records the sun on its own glass, how warm it gets, and who stops to visit. From that, by fixed rules, it draws a single image: a dial of its day. The image forms live, minute by minute, and is sealed at Bosa's sunset. After that, nothing can change it.

You can only see it there, standing in front of the panel, on your own phone, through the panel's own Wi-Fi. Each panel is also a working sundial: its shadow tells the solar time while its data draws the day.

All nine panels see the same sun. None of them sees the same day. A street that faces the sea, a wall in the shade of the castle, an alley that loses the sun by three in the afternoon: each place writes itself into its dial. **Same sun, different data.**

When the week is over, the 63 dials, nine panels by seven days, become one screen print, made in Bosa in four inks: the whole exhibition on a single sheet.

*Horas non numero nisi serenas.* I count only the sunny hours.

## Visiting

- **When:** seven days in June 2027, centred on the solstice (dates to be confirmed). The panels wake with the light and sleep after sunset; there are no opening hours but the sun's.
- **Where:** nine walls across Bosa. Scan the QR code on the flyer or on any panel's plate for the map, the route and which panels are awake now.
- **Start at the girasole:** the first stop on the map. A young sunflower turns with the sun beside a chalkboard: add your mark, ochre for sun or blue for shade, and write the time. The board is wiped at sunset, ready for the next day.
- **How:** at a panel, join its Wi-Fi (`panel-01` to `panel-09`). Its page opens by itself. Stay connected, even if your phone says there is no internet: there isn't, by design.
- **Cost:** free. Your visit is counted, never identified: it becomes a small black dot on that hour of the dial.

## Heliotropism: a short history, mostly invented

**Heliotropism** (*Eliotropismo*) is the art of turning towards the sun. Its name is borrowed from botany, where it means a plant that follows the sun across the sky: from *Helios*, the sun, and *tropos*, a turning. Its followers are the Heliotropists (*Eliotropisti*). Its motto is the old sundial line *Horas non numero nisi serenas*, and its works are made by the sun and signed by no one.

**The legend.** The Heliotropists say their first member was a sunflower. Nobody planted it. A bird dropped the seed in a crack at the foot of an old wall facing the sea, in a town by a river, under a sundial so worn that only its motto could still be read: *Horas non numero nisi serenas*.

All summer the flower did what sunflowers do. It followed the sun from east to west, and every night, in the dark, it turned back to face the east and wait. When a cloud passed, it waited. When the sun went behind the castle, it waited. It counted nothing after sunset. The sundial above it could no longer tell the time; the flower, below, told only which hours were sunny.

Someone who passed the wall every day began to draw marks on the wall behind the sunflower: one mark each quarter of an hour, one colour for sun and another for shade, each day closed at sunset and never touched again. By the end of the summer the wall was covered in unsigned days, no two alike, all made by the same sun. The flower went to seed. The marks stayed on the wall. That, the Heliotropists say, was the first exhibition.

**The quiet centuries.** For a long time Heliotropism had almost no declared members, because a Heliotropist may not claim to be one. Its history is written in the gaps: unsigned dials, shadows left to do the drawing, pinhole cans left on fences for half a year. Heliotropists recognise each other's work, never each other.

**The panels.** Heliotropism waited for a material that could see the sun and also forget it. It found one in the solar panel at the end of its life: too tired to power a house, still awake enough to notice the light. SUNDIAL · La Meridiana is, the Heliotropists claim, the movement's first exhibition since the sunflower, and also its last, until the next one. The panels stay still; the visitors do the turning, walking from wall to wall after the light.

**Heliotropists without knowing it.** The movement is far larger than its members admit. Every summer, thousands of people turn south and follow the sun to towns like Bosa: they plan their days by it, move their towels with the shade, and judge a holiday by its sunny hours. By the Heliotropists' count, every one of them is a member. Like the panels, they come back each year to the same sun, and like the panels, no two of them have the same day.

**The rules of Heliotropism**

1. The sun makes the work. The artist only writes the rules, and the rules are fixed before the first light.
2. One panel, one place, one day. Each work belongs where it was made.
3. A work is finished at sunset. Nothing after dark is counted.
4. Shade is not failure. Blue is a true record, not a missing one.
5. A day the panel did not wake is still a work: the outline of what could have been.
6. The work is seen where it is made, or not at all.
7. Visitors are counted, never named.
8. The work is signed by no one. The rules carry a version number instead.
9. Count only the sunny hours.
